Step 02 / Label-Noise Weighting

Teaching the Model
What to Trust.

A visual guide to detecting suspicious training labels, estimating sample reliability, and preparing weighted data for Transformer learning.

This phase does not train the final Transformer. Instead, it uses out-of-fold predictions from Logistic Regression models to estimate how much each training example should influence the next stage of learning.

2.86M Expected training rows
05 Product-grouped folds
10 Fresh LR models
0.1–1 Sample weight range
The central idea: Do not automatically delete suspicious reviews. Estimate their reliability and reduce their influence during future training.
01
Understanding the problem

Not Every Label Is Correct.

The dataset contains millions of customer reviews. Each review has an assigned label, but some labels may not agree with the actual meaning of the text.

What does Label Noise mean?

A label is the target category assigned to a training example. Label noise occurs when that assigned category is incorrect, unreliable, or inconsistent with the review content.

✓
REVIEW 01

"The product quality was excellent. I am really satisfied."

Recommended Apparently consistent
✓
REVIEW 02

"Terrible quality. It was absolutely not worth buying."

Not Recommended Apparently consistent
!
REVIEW 03

"Excellent! I really loved it."

Not Recommended Potential contradiction
A contradiction is a warning sign, not proof of a wrong label. A review can contain mixed opinions or context that a simple classifier cannot understand.

02
The central concept

Weight, Don't Delete.

Instead of removing uncertain examples, we assign each training review a numerical importance score.

What is Sample Weighting?

A sample weight controls how strongly a training example contributes to the learning objective.

Three hypothetical training reviews

A higher weight means a stronger contribution to training.

Review 01 — High reliability 1.00
Review 02 — Moderate reliability 0.70
Review 03 — Suspicious label 0.20
All three examples remain in the dataset. The third example simply has less influence during optimization.

Where will the weights be used?

Training Review + Corrected Label Input to the final Transformer
↓

↓
Individual Loss × Sample Weight Each sample's contribution is adjusted.
↓
Weighted Training Objective The basis for parameter updates.

Step 2 prepares the weights. It does not train the Transformer itself.

03
Data partitioning

Product-Grouped Folds.

Before estimating label reliability, we need a fair way to separate training examples from the examples used for out-of-fold prediction.

Why can't we randomly split review rows?

A product may have hundreds or thousands of reviews. If reviews from the same product appear in both training and prediction data, the model may benefit from seeing other reviews about that same product.

Incorrect split
One product appears everywhere
R1, R3 Training
R2, R4 Prediction

All four reviews belong to the same product.

Correct split
One product stays together
R1, R2, R3, R4 One grouped unit

The entire product group belongs to one fold.

What does StratifiedGroupKFold do?

G
Group

All reviews belonging to one product_id must stay in the same fold.

S
Stratified

The class proportions should be as similar as possible across folds.

Stratification is an optimization goal, not a guarantee of perfectly equal class percentages. Product-group constraints can make exact balance impossible.

Reliable prediction

Out-of-Fold Predictions.

The model must evaluate a review that it did not use during its own fitting process.

First, understand Logistic Regression

The baseline system represents review text with TF-IDF. Logistic Regression then uses those numerical features to predict class probabilities.

Raw Review Text "The quality is good, but the price is high."
↓
TF-IDF Vector A numerical representation of the text.
↓
Logistic Regression Learned class decision boundaries.
↓
predict_proba() One probability for each class.

The probabilities sum to 1. They describe the model's estimated class distribution.

Why not evaluate a model on its training rows?

Suppose a student memorizes the answers to an exam before taking it. A high score would not necessarily demonstrate understanding.

A model can also memorize patterns from examples it has already seen. If it evaluates those same examples, its predictions may look unrealistically good.

In-sample evaluation: Predicting a row with a model that was fitted using that row. This is unsuitable for our label-reliability estimation.
Out-of-fold evaluation: Predicting a row with a model that did not use that row during fitting.

05
From predictions to reliability

How Probabilities Become Weights.

The mathematical core of Step 2 combines two different signals: how well the recorded label fits its own class, and how confident the model is overall.

Step 1 — Extract the probability of the recorded label

We call this value py. It is the OOF probability assigned to the class already recorded in the dataset.

Example A

Recorded label: Not Recommended

Recommended
0.10
No Idea
0.15
Not Recommended
0.75
py = 0.75

The model assigns 75% probability to the recorded label.

Step 2 — Normalize within each class: Q

A raw probability may be unfair to naturally ambiguous classes. For example, a model may generally be less confident when predicting a middle category.

To reduce this problem, compare each example with the average probability of its own recorded class.

Illustrative class averages
Class Mean recorded-label probability
Recommended 0.80
No Idea 0.50
Not Recommended 0.75
Class-relative score
Q = min(1, py / mc)
m_c is the mean recorded-label probability across all OOF examples of class c.
Example A

Recommended sample

0.64 / 0.80 = 0.80
Q = 0.80
Example B

No Idea sample

0.40 / 0.50 = 0.80
Q = 0.80

Both examples receive the same Q because each performs equally relative to the average confidence of its own class.

Step 3 — Measure predictive uncertainty: Entropy

Q describes the probability of the recorded label relative to its class. However, it does not describe the entire probability distribution.

Consider two situations: a model strongly prefers one class, and a model distributes its probability almost equally across all three classes.

Highly confident Low entropy

Probabilities: 0.90 / 0.05 / 0.05

Highly uncertain High entropy

Probabilities: 0.33 / 0.33 / 0.34

Hnorm = − Σ pk ln(pk) / ln(3)
Probabilities are clipped below by ENTROPY_EPS before computing logarithms.

Since there are three classes, dividing by ln(3) normalizes entropy to the range from 0 to 1.

Probability distribution Normalized entropy Interpretation
1 / 0 / 0 0.00 Fully concentrated
0.70 / 0.20 / 0.10 ≈ 0.73 Partially uncertain
⅓ / ⅓ / ⅓ 1.00 Maximum uncertainty

Step 4 — Convert uncertainty into confidence

C = 1 − Hnorm
Confidence is the inverse of normalized entropy.
C ≈ 1

The model is confident and its probability distribution is concentrated.

C ≈ 0

The model is uncertain and distributes probability across multiple classes.

Step 5 — Compute the final sample weight

Final weighting equation
w = max(WMIN, 1 − C(1 − Q))
W_MIN = 0.1 in this project.

The equation combines class-relative reliability Q and model confidence C.

High Q

The sample performs well relative to other examples of its own class. Its weight tends to remain high.

Low Q

The recorded label has relatively weak probability. Weight reduction becomes possible.

Low C

The model is uncertain. The formula limits the impact of a potentially unreliable disagreement.

High C

The model is confident. A weak class-relative score can reduce the sample weight more strongly.

Interactive weight calculator

Adjust Q and C to see how the final weight changes. The minimum weight is fixed at 0.1.

0.20
0 = weak relative compatibility; 1 = strong relative compatibility.
0.90
0 = uncertain model; 1 = highly confident model.
w = max(0.1, 1 − C × (1 − Q))
0.28 Calculated sample weight

Human-in-the-loop validation

Can We Trust the Weights?

A machine-generated confidence score is not automatically a valid measure of label quality. The selected examples must be inspected by a human.

Two important acceptance questions

01
Low-weight precision

Among manually reviewed examples with weight below 0.5, how many are genuinely suspicious?

Target ≥ 70%
02
High-weight contradictions

Among manually reviewed examples with weight at least 0.9, how many contain clear contradictions?

Target ≤ 10%
These are engineering thresholds chosen for this project. They are not universal statistical guarantees or scientifically established laws.

The final human decision

Option A

KEEP

The evidence suggests that the weighting procedure is useful enough to carry forward.

Proceed with weights
Option B

DROP

The procedure is not convincing enough. The Transformer will proceed without these estimated weights.

Use w = 1

The decision is made by the user, not automatically by an acceptance threshold.

How does the Transformer use the weights?

During the next phase, the Transformer calculates an individual loss for each training example.

Lweighted = Σ(wi × Li) / Σwi
The denominator is the sum of valid sample weights, not the batch size.
Review Individual loss Weight Weighted loss
R1 0.80 1.00 0.80
R2 0.60 0.90 0.54
R3 1.20 0.20 0.24
Two separate heads: w_rec scales the Recommendation loss, while w_band scales the Rating Band loss. A missing label is masked out for its corresponding head.

Pseudo-labeled examples receive weight 1 in the next training phase. Rows without a valid label do not contribute to the corresponding head's loss.

10
Scientific interpretation

What This Method Cannot Guarantee.

The estimated weights are model-based indicators of potential label reliability. They are not ground-truth judgments about individual reviews.

Important methodological limitations

Probability calibration
Baseline Logistic Regression probabilities are not necessarily calibrated. A predicted probability of 0.8 does not automatically mean that 80% of equivalent cases are truly correct.
Ordinal information in Rating Band
The Q and C calculations treat the three classes as categorical outcomes. They do not use the natural order between rating categories.
TF-IDF fitted on the full training set
The cached vectorizer was fitted on the complete training text without using labels. Therefore, the text features are not strictly fold-specific. This is recorded as a methodological limitation.
Repeated review bodies
Identical text bodies may receive slightly different weights because their predictions can come from different fold-specific models.
Single fold seed
Only one fold seed is specified. Stability across multiple independent fold assignments is not measured in this phase.
Engineering acceptance thresholds
Thresholds are engineering assumptions defined for this project. They are not statistically validated universal boundaries.

Data integrity checks versus quality warnings

Hard validation

Assertions

These detect structural problems that make the computed output unreliable.

  • Missing OOF coverage.
  • Duplicate or misaligned IDs.
  • Invalid probability sums.
  • NaN probability values.
  • Unexpected class order.
These can stop the run.
Quality monitoring

Warnings

These indicate that the method may not behave as expected.

  • Low mean sample weights.
  • Too many low-weight rows.
  • Class-specific weight gaps.
  • Unexpected OOF F1 difference.
These do not automatically stop the run.

Important project constraints

  • Use corrected Step 1 labels throughout the project.
  • Do not use validation or test data for fitting this phase.
  • The validation reference Macro F1 may be read from the selected baseline record.
  • Do not run the additional LR weight-application experiment.
  • Do not suppress ConvergenceWarning.
  • Do not delete the TF-IDF cache without user approval.
  • Use one writer for DuckDB.
  • Keep long-running OOF execution resumable.

Essential Concepts.

A compact dictionary of the terms used throughout this phase.

Label Noise Incorrect or unreliable assigned target labels.
Sample Weight A coefficient controlling a training sample's influence.
Fold One partition of a dataset used in cross-validation.
Cross-Validation A method that repeats fitting and held-out evaluation across multiple partitions.
Stratification Preserving class proportions as closely as possible.
Grouping Keeping related samples together during splitting.
OOF Out-of-Fold: a prediction from a model that did not fit the predicted sample.
TF-IDF A numerical text representation based on term frequency and inverse document frequency.
Logistic Regression A supervised classification algorithm that can produce class probabilities.
Entropy A measure of uncertainty in a probability distribution.
Confidence Here, one minus normalized entropy.
Macro F1 The unweighted average of class-level F1 scores.
Data Leakage Information from an evaluation example improperly influencing its prediction.
Parquet A compressed, column-oriented binary format for structured data.
SHA256 A cryptographic hash used to check file identity and integrity.
Human-in-the-Loop A process in which people inspect or validate machine-generated results.
Class Weight A training adjustment that compensates for class imbalance.
Regularization (C) The inverse-strength parameter conventionally used in Logistic Regression.

The big picture

One Phase. One Purpose.

We are not asking the auxiliary model to prove which labels are correct. We are asking it to estimate how strongly each existing label is supported by the review text.

Corrected Training Labels Step 1 output
↓

↓
Ten Fresh Logistic Regression Fits Two tasks × five held-out folds
↓
OOF Probabilities Every valid sample predicted without self-training
↓

The most important takeaway: Step 2 is a label-reliability estimation and uncertainty management procedure. It is not an automatic label-correction system and it does not guarantee that every low-weight sample has an incorrect label.
